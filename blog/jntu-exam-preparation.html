How to Prepare for JNTU Exams: A Complete Guide for Engineering Students

10 min read · Guides · Back to all guides

Advertisement

JNTU exams follow a pattern that most students figure out too late — usually after their first semester results. This guide is what you should know before you start preparing, not after.

Understand the exam pattern first

JNTU semester exams are 70 marks (external) with an internal component of 30 marks. The external paper typically has two sections — Section A with short answer questions and Section B with long answers where you choose from options. Knowing this matters because it changes how you prepare: you don't need to master every topic equally. You need to cover enough topics in each unit to have choices in Section B.

Each unit in the syllabus usually maps to one question in Section B. If there are five units and you need to answer three long questions, you can strategically focus your deep preparation on four units and do lighter coverage on the fifth — and still have comfortable choices in the exam.

Start with previous question papers, not the textbook

The single most useful thing you can do at the start of exam prep is collect five years of previous JNTU question papers for each subject. Go through them before opening your notes. You will immediately see which topics appear every year, which ones appear occasionally, and which ones have never come in the last five years.

This takes two hours per subject and saves you from spending days on topics that will never appear in your exam. Most students skip this step and end up studying everything with equal effort — which is the least efficient way to prepare for a predictable exam.

Unit-by-unit planning

Once you know which topics are high-frequency, plan your coverage unit by unit. For each unit, decide: which topics get full preparation (notes, worked examples, practice answers), which get light reading only, and which get skipped entirely because they haven't appeared in five years and you have enough other options.

Write this plan down before you start studying. Most students open the textbook and start from page one without ever making this decision, which means they run out of time before they finish and end up with incomplete coverage of everything rather than solid coverage of what matters.

Five years of previous papers tells you more about what to study than the entire textbook does.

How to write answers that score well

JNTU long answers are marked partly on content and partly on presentation. A few things that consistently help:

  • Start each answer with a one-line definition or introduction. It signals to the examiner that you know what you're talking about before you go into detail.
  • Use diagrams wherever possible. A labeled diagram in a technical subject can cover three or four sentences of explanation and is faster to write. Examiners respond well to them.
  • Use headings and sub-points inside long answers. A wall of text is harder to mark than a structured answer with clear sections. Bullet points and numbered steps make your answer easier to read.
  • End with a conclusion sentence. Even one line that summarizes what you just answered signals a complete answer.

Internal marks — don't ignore them

30 internal marks come from mid-term exams and assignments. Many students treat internals as less important than the external exam, which is a mistake. A strong internal score gives you a cushion in the external — if you score 22–25 out of 30 internally, you only need 35–40 out of 70 externally to pass comfortably. Ignore internals and you need 40+ in the external just to pass.

Mid-term exams are also a useful practice run for the actual exam — same pattern, easier stakes. Use them to figure out where your time management breaks down under exam conditions.

The two weeks before the exam

  • Week 2 out: finish first pass of all units you've planned to cover. No new topics after this point.
  • Week 1 out: revision only — practice writing answers from memory, time yourself, review diagrams and definitions.
  • Day before: read through your short notes only. No heavy studying. Sleep on schedule.
