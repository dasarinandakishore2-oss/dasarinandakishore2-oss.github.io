Study Techniques That Actually Work: A Practical Guide for Exam Season

9 min read · Guides

If you've ever closed your notes after an hour of reading and felt like you knew the chapter, only to blank on the same material in the exam hall, you've experienced the gap between feeling like you've studied and actually having learned something. That gap has a name in learning research, and it's the reason re-reading is the most popular study method and one of the least effective.

Why re-reading feels productive but isn't

Re-reading creates something called fluency illusion. The material becomes familiar, and familiarity feels like knowledge. But recognizing a sentence you've read three times is a completely different mental task from producing that same information from memory with no prompt in front of you — which is exactly what an exam asks you to do. Fluency doesn't transfer to recall.

Active recall

Active recall means testing yourself on material instead of reviewing it. Close the book, and try to write or say everything you remember about a topic before checking your notes. Then check what you missed. That gap between what you thought you knew and what you actually produced is the most useful information you'll get while studying — it's a precise map of what still needs work, instead of a vague feeling of "I should probably go over this again."

How to actually do it

  • After reading a section, close it and write down everything you remember, in your own words, before looking back.
  • Turn your notes into questions as you take them, not after — "What causes X?" instead of "X is caused by Y." Answer your own questions later without looking.
  • Past papers are active recall in its most exam-relevant form. Use them earlier in your prep, not just as a final check.

Spaced repetition

Information you review once and never again fades fast — often within days. Spaced repetition means reviewing material at increasing intervals: a day later, then a few days later, then a week later, instead of cramming every review into the same evening. Each review, spaced out, resets the forgetting curve and makes the memory more durable than the last time.

In practice, this means starting a subject earlier than feels necessary and touching it briefly, repeatedly, rather than doing one long session the week before the exam. Twenty minutes of review three times over two weeks beats one hour-long session, for the same total time spent.

Interleaving

Interleaving means mixing different topics or problem types within a single study session, instead of drilling one type until you've "mastered" it before moving to the next (called blocking). It feels harder and slower in the moment — you're switching context constantly, and your immediate performance during practice actually looks worse. But it builds something blocking doesn't: the ability to recognize which method a problem needs, not just how to execute the method once you've been told which one to use.

This matters most for subjects with several distinct problem types — mixed numerical problems in physics or chemistry, for instance, where the real exam challenge is identifying the right approach, not just executing it once you know which one applies.

Interleaving feels worse while you're doing it and works better once you're tested. Trust the process over the feeling.

Fitting this into a week you don't control

The theory is well established; the harder part is fitting it around a full class schedule. A realistic approach:

  • Use small windows, not ideal ones. Ten minutes of active recall between classes is worth more than waiting for a mythical uninterrupted three-hour block that rarely arrives.
  • Review yesterday's material for five minutes before starting today's. This alone builds spacing into your routine without extra planning.
  • End each study session by writing three questions about what you just covered, to answer cold at the start of the next session. It costs two minutes and sets up your next recall session for free.
  • Protect one fixed block a day, even if it's short. Consistency at 30 minutes daily beats an irregular 3-hour session once a week — spacing depends on regularity, not total hours.

What to drop

Highlighting and re-reading aren't useless — they're fine for a first pass to get oriented in unfamiliar material. The mistake is treating them as the studying itself, rather than the warm-up before the studying (recall, spacing, mixed practice) actually starts.
